Qualitative Methods · Content Analysis

One Text, Three Contexts

In the coding activity, the finding moved when the judgment behind it moved. This time you'll move that judgment on purpose.

Krippendorff makes the claim plainly: a speech on economics can be read for what the speaker knows about economics, for the emotions it arouses, or for its political implications — and which reading you get depends on the context you choose to listen within. You're going to hold one short speech still and walk it through three of those contexts.

For each, you'll decide what the context lets you infer, what it doesn't, and whether the inference could be validated in principle. Nothing here is graded.

The text · read it once

A candidate speaks to a town that lost its mill

"Three years ago this town's mill closed, and four hundred paychecks went with it. The experts told us to be patient — that the market would correct itself, that retraining would fill the gap. Patience is a luxury for people who still have a paycheck. So we stop waiting for trickle-down promises that never reach the ground. We cut the payroll tax for every small employer on Main Street. We tie new infrastructure spending directly to local hiring. And we measure success not by the stock index but by whether the lights are on in the houses on Fourth Street. Prosperity isn't a number in a quarterly report. It's a kitchen table where nobody's afraid of the mail."

Those are the only words you'll analyze. They won't change. What changes, three times over, is the question you bring to them.

Three findings · one text

Same 120 words. Three results.

You read a single, unchanging speech three times. Here is what each context produced — every one of them defensible, none of them reducible to the others.

Read for economic competence

The speaker commands the vocabulary of economic policy, but the speech cannot establish whether he grasps how those policies would actually work.

Read for emotional arousal

The speech is built to carry an economically insecure audience from fear toward belonging, anchored in the image of a kitchen table free of dread.

Read for political strategy

The speech positions its author as a populist outsider, courting working-class and small-business voters against an expert establishment.

None of these findings was sitting in the speech waiting to be extracted. Each one is an inference, and each became available only once you chose a context to read within. Change the context and you change what the text can answer — which is the whole argument of the chapter, made operational.

Why it has to be explicit

Then state your context, or anything goes

If three valid readings can come from one text, a finding means little until the reader knows which context produced it. A claim to have analyzed "the content" of the speech hides the choice that did the real work.

So the discipline Krippendorff asks for is simple to state and easy to skip: name the context, name the research question, and make the inference validatable in principle. That's what separates a content analysis from an opinion about a text.

Two activities, one idea. The inference lives in the coder's judgment, and that judgment is governed by a context the analyst chooses. Make the context explicit, and the work becomes something another researcher can check.
