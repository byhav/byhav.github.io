Gaining Access: A Researcher's Decision Path

Five decisions. One research project. What happens when theory meets a gatekeeper.

Stage 1 of 5
Stage One

An Informal Yes

You're a doctoral student studying burnout among ICU nurses. You also work full-time at Regional Medical Center, in the IT department, where your day job has nothing to do with critical care. Your dissertation calls for semi-structured interviews with 15 to 20 ICU nurses at this hospital. IRB review is pending. Interview protocol is drafted.

Three weeks ago, at a hospital recognition dinner, you mentioned the study to the Chief Medical Officer in passing. She said, "Oh, that sounds great, definitely do it here." Nothing has happened since. No follow-up email, no formal approval, no introduction to anyone in the ICU.

You have a verbal blessing that may or may not still exist. You don't yet have access to a single nurse.

What do you do first?

Stage Two

Framing the Request

You have written confirmation from the CMO. Now you need to write to the ICU nurse manager, Dr. Hernandez, whom you've never spoken with. You're aware she's heard about your study from the CMO and is expecting an email.

The opening line will set the tone. You're also conscious that you both work for the same employer, which cuts both ways. She might be more receptive, or more skeptical of a coworker asking favors.

Which framing do you use?

Stage Three

The Gatekeeper's Hesitation

Dr. Hernandez replies the next morning:

"Thanks for the email. I'll be honest with you. I heard about your study from the CMO last week, before I heard from you, and I was a little surprised. Three other burnout studies have asked for access in the last year and we declined all of them. The unit is tired of being a research site. What would you do differently?"

How do you respond?

Stage Four

What You Ask For

Dr. Hernandez is warming up. She asks what exactly you want from her unit.

You actually want three things, eventually: interviews with 8 nurses, a focus group, and permission to observe shift handoffs. But you have no rapport yet.

What do you ask for in this reply?

Stage Five

The Nurses Themselves

You're approved. Dr. Hernandez sent an email introducing you to the ICU team. You arrive on your first scheduled day, badge in hand, IRB letter in your bag.

Two of the eight nurses on the list look uneasy when you introduce yourself. One says, "I've seen you in the cafeteria before, right? Don't you work in IT? I didn't really know what this was about. We just got the email." The other nurse doesn't say anything but doesn't make eye contact.

What do you do next?

Your decisions, mapped to the framework

Working at the organization you want to study is both an advantage and a trap. It opens doors that outsiders can't open (the CMO's casual blessing) and it makes every misstep more public, every gatekeeper more suspicious of being short-circuited, and every nurse more aware that you are simultaneously a coworker and a researcher. Access is rarely won or lost in a single move. It's a sequence of judgment calls, each informed by what Saunders and colleagues call the three levels of access (physical or virtual, continuing, and cognitive) and theories about why people choose to participate. The choices you missed above are the ones worth rereading.
